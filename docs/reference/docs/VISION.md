# 1. The One-Sentence Vision

> **DutyGraph becomes the living, AI-readable operating model of a company: it learns how work actually happens, captures the judgment that governs that work, helps humans coordinate with less friction, prepares work for software and AI agents, governs what those agents are allowed to do, routes opportunities to the right executor, and learns from the outcomes.**

The simplest conceptual loop is:

**Discover → Operate → Learn → Diagnose → Route → Execute → Measure → Update DutyGraph**

The product should become more accurate and more valuable as the company operates.

# 2. The Core Thesis

The next enterprise AI bottleneck is not model intelligence.

Models are improving quickly. Agents are proliferating. Agent frameworks, coding systems, tool-use layers, orchestration systems, and model providers will become abundant.

The scarce asset moves upward.

The scarce asset becomes:

- company-specific operating context;
- expert judgment;
- rules;
- exceptions;
- ownership;
- authority;
- evidence;
- permissions;
- the distinction between what people **did** and what they **should do**;
- a current account of how the company actually works.

The key thesis is:

> **Agents do not merely need access to enterprise data. They need approved operating judgment.**

A company may have:

- an org chart;
- SharePoint;
- Slack or Teams;
- CRM records;
- ERP records;
- tickets;
- SOPs;
- email;
- meeting transcripts;
- job descriptions;
- vendor systems;
- process diagrams;
- tribal knowledge;
- thousands of examples of work being performed.

Yet it may still be unable to answer the question an intelligent new worker or AI agent effectively asks:

> **“How does this place work, what am I allowed to do, what rules govern the work, what are the exceptions, and who decides when the rules are not enough?”**

DutyGraph exists to answer that question.

# 3. Why This Market Exists Now

For decades, maintaining a complete and current account of how a company operates was economically unrealistic.

Companies could write SOPs, process maps, training manuals, and policies, but those artifacts degraded because:

- work changes faster than documentation;
- people do not consistently document exceptions;
- no single employee knows the entire process;
- the most important judgment often remains tacit;
- organizations contain legitimate deviations alongside mistakes;
- maintaining documentation historically produced limited return because humans still had to read and remember it.

AI changes both sides of that equation.

## AI reduces the cost of maintaining operating knowledge

AI can:

- analyze thousands of cases;
- interview employees conversationally;
- extract duties and tasks;
- identify repeated patterns;
- detect contradictions;
- identify unknowns;
- propose candidate rules;
- surface exceptions;
- generate follow-up questions;
- structure human explanations;
- compare intended process with observed process.

## AI dramatically increases the value of operating knowledge

Once operating knowledge is machine-readable, it can be used by:

- employees;
- assistants;
- copilots;
- workflow systems;
- AI agents;
- coding agents;
- forward-deployed engineers;
- governance systems;
- automation engines.

What used to be "documentation" starts behaving more like **production infrastructure**.

That is why the timing matters.

# 4. The FDE Boom Is a Market Signal

The recent explosion of Forward Deployed Engineer roles is evidence that enterprises are compensating for a missing software layer.

The current pattern often looks like:

**Operator → FDE → Agent**

The operator knows the work.

The FDE:

- interviews the operator;
- studies systems;
- watches workflows;
- discovers edge cases;
- interprets conflicting examples;
- determines which behaviors are legitimate;
- translates that understanding into software or agent instructions;
- tests the result;
- returns when the business changes.

This can work extremely well, but it is expensive and difficult to scale.

The strategic opportunity is to make much more of that translation persistent and software-mediated.

A context platform might move the model toward:

**Operator → Context Layer → Agent**

DutyGraph's larger ambition is:

**Organization → DutyGraph → Humans + Agents + Software**

DutyGraph is not only trying to create instructions for one agent.

It is trying to model the operating system those instructions came from.

# 6. A New Kind of Enterprise Asset

A useful analogy is the corporate data room.

When a company is sold, important financial, legal, operational, and contractual information is organized into a structured body of knowledge because an outside party needs to understand the company.

The agentic era creates a similar need.

Every company increasingly needs an **AI-readable map of itself**.

Not because the company is being sold, but because:

- AI systems need context;
- agents need operating instructions;
- automation requires clear task boundaries;
- governance requires explicit permissions and authority;
- leaders need to know where AI is actually ready;
- FDEs need implementation context;
- business knowledge needs to survive employee turnover.

The long-term thesis is:

> **An AI-readable operating model may become as fundamental to an AI-native company as source code is to software and financial records are to accounting.**

# 8. Discovery Is the Compiler Front End

DutyGraph is not ultimately a discovery application.

Discovery is how messy business reality enters the system.

Think of DutyGraph like a compiler:

**Messy human knowledge + evidence + systems + observations  
→ structured claims  
→ validated operating graph  
→ approved operating knowledge  
→ executable Task Cards  
→ build/runtime artifacts  
→ agents/software/human workflows**

The original four-stage discovery flow remains a strong foundation.

## Phase 1: Public Research

Inputs may include:

- company website;
- filings;
- job descriptions;
- public sources;
- external data providers;
- known systems;
- organizational signals.

Outputs:

- Company Snapshot;
- hypotheses;
- likely structure;
- potential systems;
- possible roles;
- unknowns that require confirmation.

This phase creates hypotheses, not organizational truth.

## Phase 2: Executive Kickoff

Executives:

- correct public research;
- establish strategic priorities;
- define operating boundaries;
- identify key initiatives;
- identify business outcomes;
- confirm or correct leadership/roster information;
- identify what matters now.

This converts external hypotheses into a company-informed starting state.

## Phase 3: Team Discovery

Employees and managers explain:

- what they are responsible for;
- what they actually do;
- how tasks are performed;
- which systems they use;
- dependencies;
- rules;
- exceptions;
- workarounds;
- approvals;
- bottlenecks;
- judgment;
- outputs.

Voice is important because it reduces friction and captures nuance.

This phase produces:

- detailed operating graph objects;
- Task Card candidates;
- contradictions;
- missing-information requests;
- evidence links.

## Phase 4: Solution Analysis

DutyGraph analyzes the completed or sufficiently covered graph to find:

- automation opportunities;
- agent opportunities;
- process changes;
- software consolidation;
- reconfiguration opportunities;
- vendor solutions;
- human redesign;
- build opportunities.

The result is not automatically “use AI.”

DutyGraph should be solution-neutral.

# 9. The Missing Fifth Loop: Runtime Learning

Discovery is not the end.

The product becomes much more valuable when the business continuously teaches DutyGraph through normal operation.

The runtime learning loop is:

**Execute  
→ Exception  
→ Escalation  
→ Human Resolution  
→ Evidence Captured  
→ Proposed Rule / Exception Update  
→ Owner Review  
→ Evaluation  
→ Approved New Version

The deepest compounding principle is:

> **Every meaningful escalation should have the potential to improve the next case.**

That means the DutyGraph gets smarter because the business operates.

The company stops renting the learning loop through recurring consulting or FDE work and begins to own it.

# 10. Meeting OS: Meetings Become Part of the Operating Graph

One of the biggest new extensions is **DutyGraph Meeting OS**.

The insight is not merely that meetings can be improved.

The insight is that a meeting is a coordination primitive and an evidence-producing event.

Today the default pattern is:

**Calendar → Meeting → Conversation → Notes → Weak Follow-Through**

DutyGraph can turn this into:

**Business State  
→ Required Inputs  
→ Async Collection  
→ Exception Detection  
→ Live Discussion Only If Needed  
→ Decisions  
→ Actions  
→ Updated Organizational State**

This changes the meeting environment.

# 11. Async First, Live by Exception

Recurring meetings should not automatically mean synchronous meetings.

A weekly operations meeting may currently involve:

- 12 people;
- 60 minutes;
- mostly status updates;
- two issues that actually require discussion.

DutyGraph can instead send each participant a short **DutyGraph Pulse**.

Example:

> **Monday Operations Pulse**  
> Estimated time: 4 minutes  
> Complete by 10:30 AM

The voice agent asks each person questions based on their actual work and previous commitments.

Not:

> “What did you do last week?”

Instead:

> “Last Monday you said the carrier API was blocking rollout. Is that still unresolved?”

If needed:

> “What specifically are you waiting on?”

Then:

> “Who owns that?”

Then:

> “Does this change Friday's delivery date?”

The participant reviews what DutyGraph understood and submits.

After all responses arrive, DutyGraph determines:

- which updates are routine;
- which commitments were completed;
- which blockers are new;
- where contradictions exist;
- which issues require a decision;
- who actually needs to discuss them.

The result could be:

> **Live discussion required: 14 minutes**  
> Required: Matt, Jesse, Nate  
> Other participants released

That is much more valuable than simply “taking better meeting notes.”

- labor hours spent in meetings;
- meeting cost;
- time returned;
- recurring meeting cost avoided;
- participant reduction;
- duration reduction;

Employees should primarily see:

**time returned**

Leaders and advisors can see:

**labor hours and financial cost avoided**

This creates a concrete ROI layer around organizational coordination.

# 13. The Meeting Agent Is Not a Note-Taker

The AI meeting agent should be a genuine facilitator.

It needs a strong behavioral doctrine and a versioned prompt system.

Carson Tate's Chapter 14 framework from *Work Simply* is useful as a foundation for the facilitator's meeting logic.

The AI should internalize the following ideas:

- determine whether a meeting is necessary;
- identify the meeting type;
- establish purpose;
- establish desired outcomes;
- identify who is actually needed;
- establish decision authority;
- right-size the meeting length;
- keep discussion relevant;
- capture execution;
- assign responsibility;
- close the loop explicitly.

A useful framework is **POWER**:

- **Purpose**
- **Outcomes**
- **Who**
- **Execution**
- **Responsibility**

DutyGraph extends this with:

- **Evidence**
- **Coverage of What / How / Who / Why**

The public website does not need to expose this framework in detail unless it is part of an article or attributed thought leadership piece. Internally, however, it should strongly shape the voice agent.

# 14. Jev Judges; the Voice Agent Facilitates

The meeting system should separate judgment from conversation.

## Judgment layer

A bounded decision model such as Jev can answer questions like:

- Does this need to be a meeting?
- What kind of meeting is it?
- Who needs to participate?
- Is there enough information?
- Is the current discussion relevant?
- Is an answer incomplete?
- Is there a contradiction?
- Is another follow-up question worth asking?
- Is the decision ready?
- Is the meeting ready to close?

## Voice facilitation layer

The voice model translates that structured state into natural conversation.

Example internal state:

- meeting type: decision;
- outcome: choose provider;
- missing information: interruption handling test;
- current topic relevance: low;
- decision ready: false.

The facilitator says:

> “I want to stop us for a second. We're moving into implementation detail, but we still don't know whether either provider handles interruption reliably. Jesse, what do we know from the test?”

## Deterministic layer

Ordinary code should handle:

- time;
- dates;
- arithmetic;
- access control;
- permissions;
- state transitions;
- authority policies;
- version checks.

This principle generalizes far beyond meetings.

# 15. The Three-Intelligence Architecture

DutyGraph should be built around three distinct kinds of machine intelligence plus one uniquely human capability.

| Layer | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Generative Intelligence** | Understand, extract, synthesize, explain, draft, interview |
| **Judgment Intelligence** | Classify, score, assess semantic fit, identify gaps |
| **Deterministic Intelligence** | Calculate, enforce, authorize, version, execute |
| **Human Authority** | Approve what becomes organizational truth and who may act |

The operating principle is:

> **AI proposes. Judgment models assess. Code enforces. Accountable humans approve.**

No one model should silently own the company's truth.

# 16. Evidence Is a First-Class Product Concept

DutyGraph should preserve the difference between:

- what was observed;
- what was inferred;
- what a human confirmed;
- what an accountable owner approved.

Historical behavior is not automatically correct behavior.

If ten employees have historically handled a process in one way, that may represent:

- correct policy;
- an outdated policy;
- a shortcut;
- noncompliance;
- a regional exception;
- an unofficial but useful process;
- a workaround caused by broken software.

Therefore DutyGraph must not merely learn:

> **What happened?**

It must establish:

> **What should happen?**

This is one of the strongest technical and marketing distinctions in the entire product.

# 17. Preserve Contradictions Instead of Inventing Truth

Suppose three people say:

> “The CFO approves every order above $25,000.”

A fourth says:

> “Regional orders over $25,000 are approved locally.”

The wrong architecture collapses this into one LLM-generated answer.

DutyGraph should preserve:

- Claim A;
- Claim B;
- evidence supporting each;
- the contradiction;
- confidence in the contradiction;
- the owner required to resolve it.

The accountable owner may ultimately establish:

**General rule:** CFO approval required above $25,000.

**Exception:** LATAM procurement follows a regional authority matrix.

Now DutyGraph contains approved organizational knowledge instead of AI-generated mythology.

# 20. Context Capsules: Do Not Dump the Entire Company Into a Prompt

The entire DutyGraph should not be copied into every AI call.

A **Context Assembler** should construct the smallest reproducible context package required for the current action.

A context capsule might contain:

- company;
- task ID;
- Task Card version;
- actor role;
- relevant rules;
- relevant exceptions;
- systems;
- permissions;
- evidence references;
- case data.

DutyGraph should log exactly which context governed an agent action.

Then the answer to:

> “Why did the agent do that?”

does not need to be:

> “The model thought...”

It can be:

> “The agent executed Task Card 3.7 using Rules 103 and 117, matched Exception 22, and used system operation X under permission scope Y.”

That is a much stronger foundation for enterprise agent governance.

Avoid vague “AI readiness” scores produced by an LLM.

DutyGraph should build measurable coverage metrics.

Potential metrics include:

- Map Coverage;
- Evidence Coverage / Evidence Confidence;
- Solution Readiness;
- Agent Readiness;
- Execution Coverage;
- Human Escalation Rate;
- Incorrect Execution Rate;
- False-Ready Rate;
- Outcome Performance.

A concept such as **Executable Coverage** is especially valuable:

> What percentage of representative cases can be handled correctly using current, approved operating knowledge without undocumented human judgment?

Important branding note:

**“Executable Coverage” is terminology prominently used by Edra. Do not present it on the public DutyGraph website as proprietary DutyGraph terminology without deliberate brand/legal review.**

- Approved Automation Coverage.

The concept is more important than the exact label.

The metric should eventually come from representative evaluations, not model opinion.

# 22. Representative Cases, Not Vibes

Suppose Accounts Payable has 500 representative historical cases.

A proper evaluation might show:

- 412 handled correctly using the approved Task Card;
- 34 required undocumented human judgment;
- 21 lacked permissions;
- 14 contradicted the current procedure;
- 11 produced incorrect results;
- 8 lacked sufficient evidence.

DutyGraph can derive objective metrics from those cases.

This is dramatically more defensible than:

> “AI Readiness: 84%”

The website can make a broader thought-leadership point:

> **Agent readiness should be tested against real work, not generated as a score.**

# 23. The Learning Loop Is the Compounding Moat

A deployed agent eventually encounters something it cannot handle.

Instead of merely failing, DutyGraph should treat the event as a knowledge gap.

Example:

> Operating knowledge gap detected.

The human resolves the case.

DutyGraph can ask whether the event was:

- an existing rule the agent missed;
- an undocumented legitimate exception;
- a policy violation;
- a new business rule;
- a one-time case;
- insufficient information.

The resolution becomes evidence.

DutyGraph proposes a Task Card update.

The owner reviews it.

Evaluations run again.

A new version is approved.

Coverage increases.

Every agent using the Task Card benefits from what one operator taught the system.

This is the true compounding loop.

# 27. Meeting Modes

DutyGraph can eventually support three levels of meeting intelligence.

## Capture Mode

The AI:

- listens;
- transcribes;
- structures;
- proposes updates;
- does not interrupt.

## Copilot Mode

The AI privately advises the human meeting leader:

- missing decision authority;
- unanswered question;
- topic drift;
- missing owner;
- time remaining;
- unresolved contradiction.

## Facilitator Mode

The AI actively runs the meeting.

It:

- opens with purpose and outcome;
- keeps the group on track;
- asks follow-ups;
- manages time;
- parks unrelated topics;
- challenges gaps;
- confirms decisions;
- confirms responsibility;
- closes the loop.

# 28. Meeting Types Need Different Playbooks

Different meeting types should not use one generic facilitator prompt.

Useful categories include:

- informational;
- decision-making;
- problem-solving;
- brainstorming;
- team-building;
- instructional / skill-development.

Examples:

## Informational
Bias toward async delivery unless live interaction adds real value.

## Decision
Clarify the exact decision, authority, options, evidence, constraints, objections, and final rationale.

## Problem-solving
Clarify current state, desired state, evidence, causes, constraints, interventions, and experiment.

## Brainstorming
Protect divergent thinking before evaluating ideas.

## Team-building
Intervene less aggressively because human interaction is part of the outcome.

## Instructional
Capture sequence, systems, rules, exceptions, and judgment. These conversations can generate Task Card candidates.

# 32. Agent Build Packets

For agentic implementations, a richer artifact is the **Agent Build Packet**.

An FDE or engineering agent may receive:

| Artifact | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Task Card | Approved operating procedure and version |
| Process graph | Upstream/downstream relationships |
| Business rules | Explicit conditions |
| Exceptions | Known edge cases |
| Judgment points | Where semantic decisions are required |
| Decision contracts | Bounded classifications/scores/probabilities |
| Tool catalog | Systems and allowed operations |
| API contracts | Inputs and outputs |
| Permissions | Exact scopes and limits |
| Human approvals | Required authority gates |
| Evidence | Why important rules exist |
| Representative cases | Real examples |
| Evaluation suite | Expected behavior |
| Failure cases | Unsafe/incorrect outcomes |
| Escalation policy | When the agent must stop |
| KPIs | Definition of success |

The FDE no longer begins with:

> “Tell me what you do.”

The FDE begins with:

> “Here is the task, the rules, exceptions, permissions, evidence, representative cases, unresolved gaps, and evaluation suite. Build against this contract.”

That means the FDE spends more time engineering and less time performing organizational anthropology.

# 35. Governance: Authority Cannot Be Manufactured

Humans provide one thing the AI system cannot manufacture:

**Authority.**

DutyGraph should model:

- who owns the process;
- who may approve changes;
- who may initiate actions;
- which permissions an agent receives;
- where separation of duties applies;
- where dual control is required;
- when the agent must escalate;
- which version of operating knowledge authorized an action.

This creates a powerful governance principle:

> **Understanding must never quietly become permission.**

The fact that an AI can interpret a process does not mean it has authority to act.
